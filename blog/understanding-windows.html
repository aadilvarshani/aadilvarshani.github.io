Understanding Windows

While building an EDR from scratch I kept running into Windows internals I only half knew, and then finding out that the half I was missing was the part that decided whether a detection was possible at all.

So this section is notes. One short post per concept, written while I work it out rather than after I have mastered it. The audience I have in mind is someone doing detection engineering who keeps meeting these terms and wants a working mental model rather than a full driver-development course.

Each post tries to answer the same three questions:

  1. What is it, mechanically. No hand-waving.
  2. What does it let a sensor do, and what does it stop a sensor from doing.
  3. What does it let an attacker do, and what can you actually see when they do it.

Where an operating systems concept explains it better than a Windows-specific description would, I borrow it and say where from. Operating Systems: Three Easy Pieces does a lot of that work.

The longer build log lives in Building an EDR. This section is the background reading underneath it.

Probably next

No promises on order or timing:

  • Race windows. The gap the IRQL post ends on, as a category of bug in its own right. Security products are not immune to it, and there is a recent CVE that makes the point better than any hypothetical would.
  • Thread context. IRQL says what you may do. It does not say whose process you are standing in, and getting that wrong causes attribution bugs.
  • DPCs and timers. Kernel code that runs with no thread and no process behind it, which is a real blind spot for thread-centric telemetry.
  • VADs. Image-backed versus private memory, and why this is the most reliable signal for manually mapped code.
